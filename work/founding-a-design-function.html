Case study 04

Founding a design function that was told no

I put together a pitch deck for a UX practice, presented it to the head of an Air Force software unit, and was turned down. I started doing it anyway, after hours, as a volunteer. Five years later the unit had a funded design wing and I was running it.

Organisation
Israeli Air Force software unit
Dates
2012 to 2017
Role
First UX designer in the IAF, unofficially then officially. Later founded the UX wing.
Proof artifact
Aerial defense support software, 2013 to 2014

The problem I noticed

I joined at 18 and spent three years across project management, training, standards and knowledge management. From those seats I could see the same thing from several angles: roughly a thousand developers in the unit, building a great deal of software, that almost nobody fully used and nobody liked.

I taught myself user experience practice and tooling on my own time. At that point the discipline read to most people in the unit as graphic design, and design was not something a military software organisation was expected to care about.

The refusal, and what I did with it

I built the pitch and I prepared for it properly. The answer was no.

So I offered my services to individual teams as a volunteer, in my own time, under a title I gave myself. Word spread. Demand grew faster than I expected, and within months I was spending around eighty per cent of my time on product design work that was not formally my job. The unit gave me the title of its first UX and UI designer unofficially. Three years later it became official.

The function existed for three years before anyone approved it. Demand made the case that the pitch could not.

The proof: aerial defense support software

The best evidence is a system I joined after it had already shipped. The unit built and maintained the software aerial defense teams used. It carried operating principles inherited from older systems, which were not always intuitive and left room for human error, and operator response times were too long for a system where response time is the whole point.

Nobody had asked for a redesign, and there was neither the appetite nor the budget for one. I offered to join and improve usability within the constraints that existed.

Who used it

  • Operators, working shifts in a field command centre. They identify incoming threats, judge the likely damage, warn civilians in the impact zone, and launch interceptors when necessary.
  • Intelligence researchers, working from a base. They feed operators the data behind real-time decisions, and analyse system performance afterwards to improve it.

The constraints

  • Ten developers and two system architects, and I worked directly with them.
  • Legacy technology with very little flexibility.
  • No buy-in and no resources for a full redesign.

What was actually wrong

The interface paired a map with a list of sensor inputs. Estimated impact areas were drawn on the map as ellipses, each threat tied to inputs from different sensors.

  • Live threats and past threats were drawn the same way. There was no way to tell them apart at a glance.
  • Clustered threats made the map unreadable at exactly the moment it mattered most.
  • The layout was not responsive. On the smaller screens operators typically used, critical information simply was not visible.
  • Data presentation was inconsistent. Threats carry different numbers of sensor readings, so the same data point appeared in different places for different threats, and the operator had to search for it every time.

What I changed

  • Responsiveness that knows what to sacrifice. When space runs out the tables hold and the map gives way, because the table is what the decision needs.
  • A fixed position for every data point, so the same reading is always in the same place regardless of how many sensors reported.
  • Tighter use of the screen, which bought back room for the map.
  • Redrawn ellipses, so a cluster stays readable and live threats separate visually from past ones.
  • Restraint elsewhere: separator lines, selective colour, spacing. Small moves that make the screen scannable under pressure.

Results

  • Operator response times improved following the changes made during the 2014 operation, on a system where response time is directly tied to outcomes.
  • Design and development were jointly awarded a commendation of excellence for extraordinary contribution.
  • Afterwards I founded the Air Force's UX wing and secured funding in the millions to recruit and train designers into it.

What I learned

  • Small changes go a long way.
  • User experience can matter as much as any feature, and in mission-critical systems the cost of a mistake makes it matter more.
  • That this was the work I wanted to do.

What I would do differently

  • Push for a deeper research phase up front, so the changes were strategic rather than tactical. I was putting out fires.
  • Use far more whitespace.
