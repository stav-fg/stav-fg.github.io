# Strategy, and where I think the plan is wrong

Written 7 September 2026, after reading everything. The handoff brief's positioning argument is
sound. Two things underneath it are not, and one thing nobody has raised yet is more urgent than
the portfolio.

---

## 1. The job search has its own deadline, and it is the same one

Every deadline in this project is about extracting artifacts before 30 September. There is a
second clock running that the brief never mentions.

Referrals, recommendations, warm introductions and internal advocacy all decay from the day she
leaves. A colleague who would spend twenty minutes referring her in September will take three
days to answer an email in November. This is not about goodwill; it is about her being absent
from the room where her name comes up.

**Nothing about that waits for the portfolio.** She can ask for referrals into Google, Meta,
Microsoft, Amazon and Apple this month with nothing but her LinkedIn and a conversation. A referral
gets a recruiter screen. A recruiter screen buys weeks. Those weeks are exactly when the portfolio
gets finished.

The sequence in the brief is: build the portfolio, then apply. That is backwards under a layoff.
The portfolio is not a prerequisite for starting conversations, and treating it as one converts a
three-week deadline into a three-month gap on her CV.

**Recommendation:** add a parallel track that starts this week. Ask every Salesforce colleague
with a big tech connection for an introduction, before her badge stops working. Fix LinkedIn
first, because it is the only artifact that matters for that conversation, and it is currently
4.5 years stale and claims she works at Asperii.

That last point deserves emphasis. Her LinkedIn is the single highest-traffic thing she owns, it
is the artifact recruiters actually look at, and it currently says she leads the design team at a
company she left in 2022. Fixing it is an afternoon. It outranks every remaining item on the
portfolio.

## 2. The portfolio's structure is fighting its own argument

The four case studies are Salesforce, Salesforce, Webiks 2018, Air Force 2012-2017.

Read that list the way a reviewer will. Two thirds of the visible work predates 2019, and the two
recent ones are the two that are not written. Until the Salesforce studies exist, the site says
"military and enterprise designer who once did something with AI." That is the opposite of the
positioning argument.

This is already flagged in the brief as a structural note. What has changed since is that we now
know the strongest evidence in her whole record is not any of the four.

**It is the prototypes repo.** Eighty-four commits in six weeks, sole author, replacing static
Figma handoff with interactive builds on the internal design system, with agent tooling and
Figma-to-code conversion configured in the repo. Commit messages that are pixel-level design
decisions made directly in the implementation, plus a front-end bug fix. That is a designer
operating in a way most big tech design candidates cannot claim, and it is from this year.

**Recommendation:** promote it. Not as a footnote inside a Salesforce case study, but as its own
piece, and probably the first thing on the page. Something like "I stopped shipping Figma files."
It is current, it is unusual, it needs no metrics to be persuasive, and it is the only artifact
here that answers "what would you be like to work with next year" rather than "what did you do."

It also solves the dating problem. One 2026 piece at the top changes how the older work reads,
from "this is what she does" to "this is where she came from."

## 3. The missing-numbers problem is smaller than it looks, and being handled wrong

The brief calls quantification the known weakness and builds the entire September deadline around
fixing it. Worth being precise about how much that actually buys.

Numbers from a previous employer are unverifiable and every candidate has them. An interviewer at
Google cannot check whether acceptance rate went to 71%, and knows it. What they are assessing is
whether she can reason about a design decision under pressure, which is why the loop is a
portfolio review plus an app critique plus a design exercise rather than a metrics audit.

So the metrics pull is worth doing, and the deadline is real, but the *reason* is narrower than
the brief implies. Numbers do two things: they make the case study concrete, and they prove she
was close enough to the outcome to know them. Neither requires many. **Two or three real figures
across the whole portfolio is sufficient.** Twenty is not twice as good.

What actually costs her is the current state, where claims are quantitative in shape but have no
quantity: "response times have improved", "optimised shift planning". That form invites the
question and then fails it.

**Recommendation:** treat the September pull as hunting for three usable numbers, not for
everything on the list. And where nothing exists, change the sentence rather than leaving it
vague. "We never instrumented this, which I would push for now" is a strong answer. "Response
times improved" is a weak one, and they cost the same number of words.

## 4. What the site is actually for, which is not what it is currently built for

Two different readers, two different jobs, and the site currently serves neither cleanly.

A **recruiter** spends between thirty and ninety seconds. They need to know, without scrolling
carefully: senior, AI, enterprise, shipped, and where she is. That is the hero's entire job.

A **hiring manager or design reviewer** reads one case study properly and wants the reasoning:
what was hard, what got chosen against, what went wrong first. Then, if she gets to the loop, she
presents one or two of these for 45 minutes.

Those imply different things. The index page should be scannable to the point of bluntness. The
case studies should be much longer and more argumentative than they currently are, and only one
of them really has to be excellent.

**Recommendation:** stop trying to bring four case studies to the same standard. Make Service
Reply for Email genuinely deep, because it is the one she will present. Let the other three be
competent and short. The current plan spreads effort evenly across four, which produces four
adequate ones and nothing to present.

## 5. Things that are decided but probably should not be

- **Paw Pal was cut for being thin.** Agreed. But it was the only consumer-facing and only mobile
  work in the set. Every remaining piece is dense enterprise or military desktop software. For
  Google, Meta and Apple that is a narrower range than it needs to be. Not an argument to restore
  a weak project, but worth knowing that the portfolio now says "complex operational software"
  and nothing else. If range matters for a specific role, the prototype rebuild is where it can
  be bought back cheaply.
- **MSJ was never considered.** Fourteen files across five release cycles, a component library,
  vision work and gamification research. Nobody has said why it is not one of the six. It may be
  better than two of the four current case studies.
- **The Webiks slot has two candidates and the brief assumed one.** Aircraft Maintenance and
  Drill Management are both hers, both sole-designer, both written up already. Aircraft
  Maintenance is the better craft story. Drill Management is the better systems-thinking story:
  multi-branch, multi-national, user-type-driven configuration, time-travel through drill state.
  Worth a deliberate choice rather than defaulting.

---

## What I would do in the next seven days, in order

1. **Fix LinkedIn.** Highest traffic, worst state, one afternoon, unblocks everything in item 1.
2. **Ask for referrals and recommendations**, while she is still inside Salesforce.
3. **Send the metric requests**, framed as hunting for three numbers rather than forty.
4. **Interview Service Reply for Email properly**, at length, because it is the one she presents.
5. Everything else.

Note that only item 3 is on the current critical path in the brief, and items 1 and 2 are not in
the plan at all.
