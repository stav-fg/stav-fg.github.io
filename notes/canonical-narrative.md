# The canonical narrative

What the portfolio argues. Everything written for it should serve this, and anything that does not
serve it should be cut, however good it is.

---

## The claim, in one sentence

**Stav designs the line between what a machine decides and what a person decides, in software
where being wrong costs something.**

## The claim, in a paragraph

She has spent fourteen years on systems where mistakes are expensive: aerial defense, aircraft
maintenance, dialysis scheduling. When AI arrived in her area at Salesforce, the question she had
been answering all along became the central question of the field. How much authority does the
machine get? Who decides that? What does a person see before they sign their name to something a
model produced? She has now answered that at four points on one scale, in production, for
enterprise support.

## Why this is the right narrative

Three reasons, and the third is the one that matters.

**It is true.** Every claim in it is already evidenced. Nothing needs inventing.

**It is unusual.** Most designers working on AI arrived when AI arrived. She arrived with fourteen
years of designing for consequence, which is a different qualification and a rarer one.

**It converts her weakness into the argument.** The portfolio's known problem is that most of the
visible work predates 2019. Under this narrative that stops being a gap and becomes the setup. The
old work is not filler she has to explain away. It is where the instinct came from.

## The four Salesforce case studies are one argument

Told separately they are four AI features, which is a list. Told together they are a position.

| | Case study | The question | Where authority sits |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Real-Time Semantic Classification | Which model do we trust, and what does it get to fill in? | A person **trains and chooses** |
| 2 | KGER, multi-intent email reply | Will someone put their name on what a machine wrote? | The model drafts, a person **decides** |
| 3 | A3 Registry / Record Companions | What is the machine allowed to touch at all? | People **govern** it up front |
| 4 | My Service Journey | How do you persuade an organisation to change? | The range piece |

The first three are one continuous scale. The fourth is there because everything else in the
portfolio is someone at a console, and a designer who can only do that is a narrower hire.

## What the older work contributes

**Founding a design function that was told no.** The origin. It explains where the instinct for
consequence comes from and proves she builds things that do not exist yet. It is also the only
piece nobody else can copy.

**Aircraft maintenance.** Craft under constraint. Sole designer, real field research, a hostile
environment, and a system where the paperwork had outgrown the work.

**The prototypes repo.** Method rather than product. It answers "what would she be like to work
with next year", which is the question a hiring manager is actually asking.

## The three claims underneath, each already backed by fact

1. **She builds design functions where none exist.** Pitched a UX practice, was refused, did it
   as a volunteer until it became her job, then founded and funded the wing. Hired and led three
   designers at Asperii. Replaced Figma handoff with running prototypes at Salesforce.
2. **She has four years shipping production AI to enterprise customers.** Not tool fluency. Shipped
   features, six engineering teams, three countries, at one point the only designer across all six.
3. **She owned accessibility.** A differentiator most AI-focused designers lack.

## What we never do

- Claim a number we do not have. Where nothing was measured, say so. A stated absence survives a
  follow-up question and a vague quantitative claim does not.
- Name the anchor customer, the roadmap, internal service names, or the competitor assessment.
- Assert AI fluency in prose. Show the repo instead.
- Say "passionate", "leveraging", "robust", "delve", "testament", "tapestry", "unleash", "it is
  important to note", "in conclusion".

## Voice rules, written after auditing my own copy and finding it slopped

These are the specific failures found on the live site, not general advice.

**No triplets.** "Dense operational systems, real consequences, users who are busy and under
pressure" is a rhythm, not a thought. Rhetorical threes are the loudest machine tell there is. A
list of three real things is fine; three adjectives arranged for cadence is not.

**No X-not-Y.** "The hard part is not the model. It is what a person does when the model is
wrong." Also banned in the brief, and I used it on three separate pages.

**Say each thing once, in one place.** The "the model is never the interesting part" line appeared
on the home page and the About page in nearly identical form. Repetition across pages reads as
generated.

**Vary the sentence length.** Every paragraph being two or three medium sentences is the most
recognisable tell of all. Let some sentences be four words.

**Do not close every section with an aphorism.** One earns its place. Four is a formula.

**Prefer the flat statement.** "I owned accessibility for the area" is better than anything more
decorated. Understatement reads as confidence.

**Keep the specific and slightly awkward detail.** The dog and the cat. Twenty countries, no
laptop. Those are the parts no model would generate, and they are what make the rest believable.

## The one-line version, for the hero and for a recruiter's thirty seconds

> I design software for people who can't afford to get it wrong.

Everything else on the site is evidence for that sentence.
